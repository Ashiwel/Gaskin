# Follow Nigerian mothers home from the clinic

In Nigeria's 2023–24 Demographic and Health Survey, 62.5% of recent mothers had seen a skilled provider during pregnancy, but only 43.3% gave birth in a health facility and 42.8% had a postnatal check within two days. In Kebbi the facility share was 8.8% ([NDHS 2023–24 Key Indicators Report](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PR157/PR157.pdf)). That fall between the antenatal clinic and the sixth postnatal week, in the country with the most maternal deaths in the world (about 75,000 in 2023 on UN estimates, although Nigeria's own survey implies fewer), is the opportunity for an open-source continuity-of-care planner. The opening is real but narrow. Mother-facing WhatsApp and SMS chatbots are already plentiful in Nigeria, and Jacaranda Health's trial-tested PROMPTS service began enrolling mothers in Kano in March 2026, yet none of the 25 or so ventures and programmes reviewed for this report publicly offers one personalised plan, shared by a mother and her community health worker from conception to day 1,000, with a graded library of traditional practices. The three named startups all exist and all belong to UNICEF's first femtech cohort: DawaMom (Zambia) is the closest analogue, HLlama (Togo) the closest on channel, and VivaMama (India) covers only the postpartum months. The best randomised evidence promises gains of three to seven percentage points in antenatal and postnatal attendance, which should set both expectations and the primary outcome. The paying customer will be a state primary health care board or a donor programme such as the federal MAMII initiative or the US$25m MTN–Gates Multiplier, because households already meet 71–77% of health spending from their own pockets. Regulation is manageable if the mother-facing service remains informational and the health-worker module is treated as software as a medical device. UNICEF's deadline is 11 November 2026, six days away, and its form demands a live product demo and a public code repository. The recommended sequence is Kaduna first, northern Ghana within a year, and Kenya only as a module inside its government community health app.

## Nigeria loses its mothers between the antenatal clinic and the sixth week

Nigeria records more maternal deaths than any other country. The UN inter-agency group estimates about **75,000 deaths in 2023, 28.7% of the world's total**, at a ratio of **993 per 100,000 live births** ([summary of MMEIG 2025](https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/trends-in-maternal-mortality-2000-2023-report); [Intelpoint](https://intelpoint.co/insights/nigeria-has-led-global-maternal-mortality-since-2021-with-993-deaths-per-100000-in-2023/)). Both figures come from secondary summaries, because the notes did not open the UN country table. Nigeria's own verbal-autopsy survey puts the ratio at **572 (95% CI 444–699)**, statistically unchanged since 2008 ([NVASA policy brief](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PB16/PB16.pdf)), and the health minister cited "almost 57,000" deaths in 2024 ([Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/?p=2376953)). The estimates disagree on the level and agree on the trend, which is flat. Haemorrhage, mostly after birth, causes **38%** of maternal deaths, abortion-related complications 15%, and hypertensive disorders 11%, and **39% of the women who died had received no antenatal care at all** ([NVASA](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PB16/PB16.pdf)). Newborns have fared no better. Neonatal mortality was **41 per 1,000** in 2023–24 against 39 in 2018, only 13% of newborns were checked within an hour of birth, under-five stunting rose from 37% to 40%, and exclusive breastfeeding stands at 28.8% ([KIR](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PR157/PR157.pdf)).

Continuity fails at every handover. Nationally, 62.5% of women receive skilled antenatal care, 52.4% make four or more visits, 43.3% deliver in a facility, and 42.8% of mothers and 42% of newborns are checked within two days. In the North-West the same chain reads 49.0%, 40.6%, 21.9%, and 24.9% ([KIR Table 12](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PR157/PR157.pdf)). Several northern states show the antenatal-to-birth drop in its starkest form: **in Jigawa, 76.0% of women saw a skilled provider in pregnancy but 21.4% delivered in a facility, and in Kaduna the figures are 70.0% and 25.9%** ([KIR](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PR157/PR157.pdf)). These women were known to the health system and were lost before labour. The government's routine data add that about 60% of women begin antenatal care after 20 weeks ([2025 State of Health of the Nation, via NAN](https://nannews.ng/nigeria-records-maternal-health-gains-in-2025-report/)). The survey's postnatal measure counts checks by community health workers and traditional birth attendants, so the 43% figure probably overstates clinically meaningful care. Lagos, by contrast, reports 85.8% facility births and 87.3% postnatal checks. The market therefore divides into a northern problem of reach, where most births happen at home, and a southern problem of quality and follow-up.

The workforce explains why a planner must be built for community cadres. Nigeria has about **6 midwives per 10,000 people** and a shortage of some 30,000 midwives ([NANNM](https://nannm.com.ng/over-75000-nurses-midwives-left-nigeria-in-five-years-nannm/)). More than 57,000 nurses and midwives left between 2017 and 2022, a Kano midwife earns about ₦76,540 (US$48) a month, and 80% of surveyed midwives report extreme overload ([Think Global Health](https://thinkglobalhealth.org/article/economics-behind-nigerias-midwife-exodus)). The primary-care frontline is instead made up of **223,802 licensed community health practitioners**, with Kaduna (11,973) and Kano (11,382) among the largest pools ([SOHN 2025 via Premium Times](https://allafrica.com/stories/202603110087.html)). Below them are CHIPS agents, volunteers who visit homes about three days a week, keep paper household registers and 1,000-day checklists, and receive a stipend of **₦5,000 a month**, sometimes nine months late ([Nigeria Health Watch](https://nigeriahealthwatch.com/articles/torchlight/chips-connecting-communities-to-primary-health-care-facilities-in-nasarawa-state/)). In Kaduna, **77% of CHEWs, CHOs, and JCHEWs already work beyond their formal scope, but only 44% are trained in the shifted tasks** ([Pathfinder, July 2025](https://www.pathfinder.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/TSTS-Policy-Brief_July-2025.pdf)). Facilities are thin too. Of 34,076 registered primary health centres, 6,815 are functional and about 700 offer basic emergency obstetric care against an estimated need of 1,936 ([NVASA](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PB16/PB16.pdf)).

Policy now points in the product's direction. The federal Maternal Mortality Reduction Innovation Initiative (MAMII) targets **172 local government areas that account for up to 55% of maternal deaths**, and names "continuity of care through referrals and follow-ups" and digital health among its six pillars ([Nigeria Health Watch](https://nigeriahealthwatch.com/articles/thought-leadership/mamii-a-promising-initiative-to-crash-maternal-mortality-in-nigeria/)). It had enrolled and tracked **223,784 women across 33 states** by the first quarter of 2026, without naming any digital tool ([allAfrica, April 2026](https://allafrica.com/stories/202604220158.html)), and claims maternal deaths in its areas fell 17% ([allAfrica 2025 review](https://allafrica.com/stories/202512310097.html)), a programme-reported figure that LSTM is evaluating. The Nigeria Digital in Health Initiative, endorsed in November 2024, calls for an interoperable services network and a health information exchange ([ITEdgeNews](https://www.itedgenews.africa/nigeria-embarks-on-nationwide-digital-health-reform-to-boost-access-efficiency-and-innovation/)), and DHIS2 is the national reporting backbone, although a Gombe study found it under-recorded maternal events by 10–60% against facility registers ([LSHTM IDEAS](https://ideas.lshtm.ac.uk/can-we-use-dhis2-data-to-monitor-maternal-and-newborn-health-a-case-study-from-gombe-state-nigeria/)). No national electronic pregnancy register was found.

Kenya's neonatal mortality has not moved in eight years, and in Mandera, Tana River, and Turkana close to half of births still happen at home ([Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/news/gender/ten-years-on-status-of-maternal-healthcare-in-counties-4346194)). Ghana's two official sources disagree on skilled birth attendance (83.6% or 87.6%) and on institutional mortality for 2023–24, so those figures need checking against the final reports.

## Digital programmes raise attendance by three to seven points, rarely more

The strongest randomised trial of a direct-to-mother service is Jacaranda Health's PROMPTS in Kenya: two-way SMS with stage-based messages, reminders, and an AI-triaged helpdesk staffed by nurses. A cluster trial across 40 facilities and **6,139 women** found that it raised the share completing four antenatal visits by **3.1 percentage points** and guideline-recommended postnatal care by **7.4 points**, with a 2.1-point rise in care-seeking for postpartum danger signs and no effect on exclusive breastfeeding ([medRxiv preprint, 2024](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.03.24308340v1.full-text)). The trial was not powered for mortality and its outcomes were self-reported. Jacaranda's own marketing claims larger effects without stating the design ([Rockefeller Foundation](https://www.rockefellerfoundation.org/grantee-impact-stories/ai-meets-motherhood-to-bridge-the-information-gap-in-africa/)). Sources disagree on the cost, which is US$0.74 per participant in the trial paper and about US$2 per user in a 2026 NPR report that may include language-model and nurse costs ([NPR/WGCU](https://www.wgcu.org/2026-09-17/to-prevent-deaths-in-childbirth-kenyan-moms-turn-to-an-ai-powered-chatbot)). They also disagree on how much the AI handles alone: 86% of cases in the trial paper, about 70% according to Rockefeller, while NPR says 18 nurses audit answers around the clock.

One-way broadcasting does worse. India's Kilkari sent 72 weekly voice calls from pregnancy to the child's first birthday, and an individually randomised trial of **5,095 women** found no effect on its primary outcome, exclusive breastfeeding (RR 1.04), a small rise in reversible contraception (RR 1.12), and a slightly lower rate of immediate breastfeeding. Only 31% of women listened to three-quarters of the content they answered, and **44% changed their SIM card** during the study ([BMJ Global Health, 2021](https://gh.bmj.com/content/6/Suppl_5/e008838.full)). Ghana's Mobile Midwife delivered less than a third of its expected messages, and exposure fell from about 25% in the first trimester to about 6% at six to twelve months postpartum ([BMC Medical Informatics, 2017](https://bmcmedinformdecismak.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12911-017-0421-9)). Nigeria's own Hello Mama sent 5.9 million messages to 26,000 women in Cross River in English and Pidgin, coinciding with a rise in facility delivery from 40.4% to 52.6%, but it died after the donor left in 2018 ([Nigeria Health Watch](https://sjai.nigeriahealthwatch.com/?p=4290)).

Two results suggest where AI adds most. In ARMMAN's mMitra programme in India, a model that chose which of 34,453 mothers should receive one live call from a health worker reversed the usual decline in listenership (+3.6% against −8.5% in controls) and raised postpartum calcium intake by 28% ([ARMMAN and Google](https://sarveshvgharat.github.io/papers/Beyond%20Listenership:%20AI-Predicted%20Interventions%20Drive%20Improvements%20in%20Maternal%20Health%20Behaviours.pdf)). The AI did not counsel anyone, and its job was to direct scarce human attention. The only large study reporting a fall in mortality came from Noora Health's in-hospital training of mothers and relatives before discharge, followed by WhatsApp: neonatal mortality fell from 41.3 to 33.6 per 1,000 (RR 0.82) across 28 Indian hospitals, although the design was quasi-experimental ([PLOS Global Public Health, 2023](https://doaj.org/article/59ab285936544e88a828a95d6094aab0)). Decision support for health workers improves process more than outcomes. Palestine's eRegQual trial of a DHIS2 registry with clinical decision support raised appropriate anaemia screening and management (OR 1.88) and hypertension management (OR 1.62), with no change in adverse outcomes (OR 0.99) ([Lancet Digital Health, 2022](https://doaj.org/article/d0927e32238b40b0b9884f7d6ea02f89)), and the Safe Delivery App improved Ethiopian midwives' skills while its perinatal mortality difference (14 against 23 per 1,000) was not significant ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/894543)). Nigeria has its own mental-health evidence: in the EXPONATE trial, problem-solving therapy from ordinary maternal care staff doubled exclusive breastfeeding among depressed mothers (19% against 10%) ([British Journal of Psychiatry, 2019](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/product/35E0F1E403F89FAB0E15FDA0A5989486)).

The failure modes are well documented. Programmes lose women when SIMs change, when helplines go unused (only 6% of MomConnect users touched its helpdesk), when the system cannot record a new number, and when donors leave ([BMJ Global Health, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5922496)). Babyl Rwanda reached about 2.4 million registered users and then collapsed with its British parent ([The EastAfrican](https://www.theeastafrican.co.ke/tea/science-health/uk-firm-babylon-s-woes-impact-rwandan-healthcare-4340318)). Language models add their own risks. A 2026 evaluation of a WhatsApp maternal chatbot in India caught 95.6% of emergencies needing immediate care but only **77.8% of cases needing same-day review**, which accounted for most misses, and an LLM acting as judge agreed poorly with clinical experts ([arXiv 2603.13168](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13168v1)). Medical models also show a Western bias against African traditional remedies and are easily swayed by misleading cultural cues ([Africa Health Check, EMNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/emnlp/2025.emnlp-main.1639.pdf)). No digital maternal-health trial in Nigeria was found. The lesson for a Nigerian planner is to promise modest gains, measure postnatal attendance as the primary outcome, and spend the AI budget on prioritising human follow-up and on language rather than on open-ended advice.

That fact cuts both ways: UNICEF already funds their ideas, so a Nigerian applicant has to show what it adds.

**DawaMom** (searched as "Dawamom") is the product of Dawa Health, led by Dr Tafadzwa Kalisto Munzwa. It combines a retrieval-based chatbot answering from an expert-verified dataset in Shona, Ndebele, Nyanja, Tonga, Bemba, Lozi, and English with community health worker visits, last-mile logistics, and diagnostic AI, including a cervical-screening model claimed at above 96% accuracy ([UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/blog/removing-barriers-womens-health); [Africa Prize 2026](https://africaprize.raeng.org.uk/2026-cohort/tafadzwa-kalisto/)). In 2022 its agents earned 30% of the subscriptions of the women they enrolled ([MIT Solve](https://solve.mit.edu/solutions/64000)), a model that may have changed since. Its founding year and current pricing were not found, and its "tailored weekly information" reads as stage-based content rather than an adaptive plan. **HLlama** (searched as "Hllama"; the official spelling is HLlama) is built by Umbaji, a female co-founded data-science firm founded in 2023 ([UNICEF Venture Fund](https://www.unicefventurefund.org/company/umbaji)). It routes emergencies, violence, or distress to helplines and clinics through a Togolese NGO, and its method of collecting training data from native speakers rather than by machine translation is the best regional template for Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo, and Pidgin. Whether its maternal agents follow the pregnancy timeline is unknown. **VivaMama** (searched as "Vivamama") answers spoken Hindi questions, hands harder cases to a trained person, and tracks recovery with a postnatal index covering healing, mood, lactation, nutrition, and vital signs. Its founder, a doctor, is not named, and no user numbers are published ([UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/blog/supporting-new-mothers-where-and-when-its-needed)).

Inside Nigeria the mother-facing market is crowded. HelpMum claims more than 180,000 users of its WhatsApp bots, built on fine-tuned open-source models that clinicians preferred in 78% of evaluated cases ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202510240001.html)). Solayo's Moma runs from early pregnancy to the first birthday on WhatsApp, Babymigo once reported more than 200,000 users (no traction since 2022 was found) ([Disrupt Africa](https://old.disruptafrica.com/2022/08/09/how-nigerias-babymigo-is-increasing-access-to-maternal-health-information-to-save-lives/)), and mDoc's Kem has Gates money to add GPT-4. mDoc's "NurtureHer", sometimes attributed to it, could not be confirmed. Most serious is PROMPTS, which Jacaranda and eHealth Africa launched in Kano in March 2026 in Hausa and English, aiming at 25,000 mothers enrolled through community health workers ([eHealth Africa](https://ehealthafrica.org/press-release/jacaranda-health-ehealth-africa-target-25000-mothers-ai-powered-maternal-health-support-nigeria/)). The health-worker layer is thin and mostly run by NGOs: HACEY's device kit in Oyo, a Gates-funded app for traditional birth attendants in Bayelsa ([Gates Grand Challenges](https://gcgh.grandchallenges.org/grant/integrating-traditional-birth-attendants-strengthen-regulation-and-improve-quality-maternal)), and Helium Health's Gates grant for clinician-side "care plans based on early risk identification", whose outcome was not found ([Gates Foundation](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/about/committed-grants/2022/11/inv-046250)). The **MTN–Gates Nigeria Maternal Health Multiplier**, announced in September 2026, will spend about US$25m to 2030 on AI decision support for 5,000 frontline workers and 500,000 women, aligned with MAMII, and had named no implementing partners ([MTN](https://www.mtn.com/mtn-group-foundation-and-gates-foundation-launch-first-of-its-kind-maternal-health-multiplier-platform-to-expand-access-to-ai-enabled-maternal-health-care-in-nigeria/)). It could become the product's largest customer or its best-funded rival.

Kenya's incumbent is the state. eCHIS, built on Medic's open-source Community Health Toolkit, is used by more than 100,000 community health promoters on 110,000 government smartphones, and had identified 161,000 pregnant women by July 2024 ([The Standard](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/health/health-science/article/2001498549/health-ministry-rolls-out-electronic-system-to-boost-services)). Kenyan startups such as Malaica, which links mothers to midwives for a personalised journey to delivery, are small and undisclosed ([The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/big-read/2025-02-26-startups-close-gaps-in-healthcare-of-women)). In Ghana, PROMPTS runs as "Nurse Mary" with the Ghana Health Service and enrolled more than 7,000 mothers at Greater Accra Regional Hospital ([GNA, July 2026](https://gna.org.gh/2026/07/ghs-jacaranda-deploy-ai-tool-for-maternal-care/)), while the north hosts grant-funded pilots such as LifeLynk, UHAS's ENCOMPAS, Maame-Dromo, and M'enaApp, each with CAD 250,000 ([Grand Challenges Canada](https://www.grandchallenges.ca/2024/innovative-pilot-projects-to-transform-maternal-and-newborn-health-in-ghana/)), plus the prize-winning Omaya Care for postpartum calls ([GNA](https://gna.org.gh/2026/08/omaya-care-wins-innovative-health-solution-prize-at-inaugural-ghana-ai-innovation-challenge/)).

Three things are missing from every offering found. None carries a **single longitudinal plan per mother-child pair**, visible to the mother by SMS, WhatsApp, or voice and to her health worker as decision support, that updates from conception to the second birthday. None treats traditional practices as content to be graded; the only visible work treats birth attendants as referral agents. And none describes a loop in which clinician corrections update the plans, although VivaMama, mDoc, and PROMPTS all keep humans behind the bot. A mother-only chatbot would be a me-too product in Nigeria. A shared plan with a graded practice library and a health-worker workflow would be new.

## WHO has already written most of the clinical rulebook

The clinical core does not need inventing. WHO's 2016 antenatal guidance sets a minimum of **eight contacts, at up to 12 weeks and then at 20, 26, 30, 34, 36, 38, and 40 weeks**, and a package that includes daily iron (30–60 mg) and folic acid (400 µg), calcium (1.5–2 g) where diets are low in it, intermittent preventive malaria treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine from the second trimester at least a month apart, tetanus vaccination by history, HIV testing, and one ultrasound before 24 weeks ([WHO ANC overview](https://content.sph.harvard.edu/wwwhsph/sites/2413/2018/02/ANC-OverviewBriefer-letter.pdf)). Low-dose aspirin (75 mg) for women at high risk of pre-eclampsia was added in 2021 ([BIGG-REC](https://bigg-rec.bvsalud.org/en/recommendations/762d64c70e1051be68b26bca8b01ce920b0951f0)). WHO's 2022 postnatal guidance requires **four postnatal contacts, within 24 hours, at 48–72 hours, at 7–14 days, and at six weeks**, universal screening for depression and anxiety with a validated tool, a newborn danger-sign check at every contact, supine sleep, a bath delayed by at least 24 hours, and chlorhexidine on the cord where harmful substances are commonly applied. It also endorses home visits and, in some contexts, digital messages targeted at clients ([WHO PNC 2022](https://e-lactancia.org/media/papers/WHO_recommendations_on_maternal_and_newborn_care_for_a_positive_postnatal_experience.pdf)). Nigeria adopted the eight-contact model by 2018 and aligns its malaria-in-pregnancy rules with WHO ([MCSP Nigeria profile](https://www.mcsprogram.org/wp-content/uploads/dlm_uploads/2018/09/Nigeria-MIPCountryProfile.pdf)).

The machine-readable version also exists. WHO's Digital Adaptation Kit for antenatal care specifies personas, workflows, a coded data dictionary, decision tables, scheduling logic, and indicators with stable identifiers, and its FHIR implementation guide encodes the logic in CQL ([WHO DAK](https://www.aidsdatahub.org/sites/default/files/resource/who-digital-adaptation-kit-antenatal-care-2021.pdf); [WHO SMART ANC guide](https://build.fhir.org/ig/WorldHealthOrganization/smart-anc/documentation.html)). It supplies a ready list of **13 pregnancy danger signs** that trigger urgent referral, from vaginal bleeding to visual disturbance ([value set](https://build.fhir.org/ig/WorldHealthOrganization/smart-anc/ValueSet-anc-b5-de48-choices-grouper.html)). Two features suit this product. Referral and community follow-up are still marked "future work" in the guide, which leaves the continuity layer unwritten, and DHIS2 has already implemented the kit as an antenatal registry ([DHIS2 docs](https://docs.dhis2.org/ar/implement/health/rmncah/antenatal-care-registry/design.html)). The Community Health Toolkit expresses the same logic as health-worker tasks with due dates, visibility windows, and priorities, for example postnatal visits due on days 7 and 14 after a delivery report ([CHT documentation](https://docs.communityhealthtoolkit.org/building/tasks/tasks-js/)). The child years are covered by Nigeria's routine schedule, published in an NPHCDA FHIR guide, which adds the malaria vaccine at 5, 6, 7, and 15 months and vitamin A at 6 and 12 months ([NPHCDA immunisation guide](https://build.fhir.org/ig/Nigeria-FHIR-Community/NPHCDA-ImmunizationIG/health-interventions-recommendations.html)), and by WHO's 2023 complementary-feeding guidance ([ENN summary](https://ennonline.net/fex/72/who-guideline-for-complementary-feeding-of-infants-and-young-children)). For young infants, WHO's 2024 algorithm gives numeric thresholds such as temperature of 38°C or above and breathing of 60 a minute or more in the first week ([WHO 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK610598/)).

Mental health is unusually well grounded in Nigeria. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale has been validated in Nigerian women in late pregnancy, with a cut-off of 10 for any depression (sensitivity 86.7%, specificity 91.5%) and 12 for major depression ([Adewuya et al.](https://www.mentalhealthdataprizeafrica.aphrc.org/repository/paper/35053/validation-of-the-edinburgh-postnatal-depression-scale-as-a-screening-tool-for-depression-in-late-pregnancy-among-nigerian-women)), and WHO's Thinking Healthy manual gives a CBT model that community workers can deliver ([PAHO/WHO](https://www.paho.org/en/news/5-7-2016-thinking-healthy-manual-psychosocial-management-perinatal-depression)). Facility studies put postpartum depression at 19.0% in Abuja and 33.7% in Ilorin ([Pan African Medical Journal](https://www.panafrican-med-journal.com//content/article/54/14/full); [BMC Women's Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41840387/)).

Several pieces of the rulebook remain unverified and must be sourced before they enter a rules engine. The primary text of the **2015 National Standing Orders for CHEWs and JCHEWs could not be retrieved**, so the list of drugs each cadre may give is unconfirmed. Published studies say the orders tell community workers to refer hypertension, that some do not feel authorised to give antihypertensives, and that some in Ogun cited diazepam instead of magnesium sulphate for convulsions ([Ajisegiri et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1038062); [Sotunsa et al.](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s12978-016-0218-9.pdf)). Numeric blood-pressure thresholds, Nigeria's adoption of 7.1% chlorhexidine gel, the national IYCF and iCCM booklets, and WHO's postpartum haemorrhage bundle are flagged in the notes as unverified. Sickle-cell disease is a live point of conflict: WHO's first guideline on it in pregnancy (June 2025) says women taking sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine should take 400 µg of folic acid ([AlignMNH summary](https://www.alignmnh.org/?p=22114)), while Nigerian practice is believed to use 5 mg with other malaria prophylaxis, a claim the notes could not verify. That rule needs a Nigerian clinical advisory board before launch.

## Traditional practices need grading component by component

Most harm from traditional practice comes from a specific component of a custom, such as what is applied, how hot, or how much salt, and the evidence supports a graded library rather than a list of prohibitions. The table below condenses the Nigerian evidence.

| Practice | Where documented | Grade | Evidence quality | What the plan should say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prelacteal feeds, early water, discarding colostrum | National, 55–66% of infants (DHS 2003–13) | Harmful | National survey plus global evidence | First feed at the breast within an hour; colostrum as the baby's "first medicine"; involve grandmothers |
| Ash, cow dung, herbs, shea butter, or hot compress on the cord | Bauchi and Sokoto | Harmful | Household surveys, case report, Cochrane | Chlorhexidine gel or clean dry care |
| Clay eating (nzu, calabash chalk) | 25.8% of antenatal women in Anambra practise pica | Harmful | 92.3% of Nigerian clay samples exceed WHO lead limits | Check haemoglobin; offer alternatives for nausea |
| Herbs taken to speed labour | West Africa | Harmful | Survey of 214 Ghanaian midwives | Discourage, asked without judgement |
| Agbo and other decoctions in pregnancy | 21.7% of an Ibadan cohort | Potentially harmful, composition unknown | Cohort found no outcome association; class risks | Record use; never a substitute for malaria prevention or iron |
| Avoiding eggs, snails, and meat | About 37% avoid some foods (Enugu) | Harmful where protein or iron is lost | Cross-sectional, high anaemia context | Offer acceptable substitutes |
| Wankan jego (hot baths, hot bed, kanwa salt) | Hausa North | Contested | 1974 ecological study against a null 2017–18 case-control registry | Keep warmth and rest, limit salt, treat breathlessness as urgent |
| Abdominal binder after caesarean | Widespread | Supported | Meta-analysis of 13 RCTs | Encourage, not too tight |
| Moringa leaf for milk | Widespread | Supported, low to moderate certainty | Systematic reviews of RCTs | Acceptable after checking latch and feeding |
| Pap, kunu, pepper soup, leafy soups for milk | Widespread | Plausible but untested; positive as food | Ghanaian cereal-drink RCT as analogue | "Traditional use, not yet tested in women" |
| Omugwo (family postpartum care) | South-east | Plausible benefit | Indirect only | Treat the grandmother as a member of the care team |
| Birth attendants delivering babies, or acting as companions | About half of women use them | Harmful against skilled care; supported as companion and referrer | Qualitative and survey | Link attendants to the primary health centre |
| Complementary food before six months | North-West | Harmful | WHO guidance plus qualitative work | Counsel exclusive breastfeeding |

Sources: [Agho et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27483309/); [Abegunde et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29121870/); [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/simple-antiseptic-can-reduce-newborn-infections); [Ezemenahi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37768267/); [Odesa et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41739270/); [Alhassan et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39198836/); [Adeoye and Etuk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36670404/); [Ekwochi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26818243/); [Karaye et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31990449/); [Lin et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39793406/); [Ammar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40724308/); [Tahiru et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40855498/); [Iwuagwu](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38577519/); [Yalley and Sanusi Gumbi](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42232019/); [Shittu et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41748805/).

Wankan jego shows why a binary label would mislead. In 1974 researchers in Zaria proposed that hot baths, hot beds, and large amounts of kanwa salt caused the peripartum heart failure that was common in Hausa areas ([Davidson et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4549486/)). The PEACE registry, covering **406 patients in 22 Nigerian hospitals**, found that customary hot baths (OR 1.24) and salt-enriched pap (OR 1.20) were not significant risk factors, while lack of schooling, unemployment, underweight (OR 13.43), and pre-eclampsia (OR 9.01) were ([Karaye et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31990449/)). A later Kano analysis found selenium deficiency in 84.9% of patients against 3.3% of controls ([Karaye et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38583094/)). The registry had only 99 controls, so a modest effect could have been missed. The useful message for northern mothers therefore concerns nutrition and breathlessness after birth, and the warm bath itself need not be discouraged. The same care applies to omugwo: it is plausibly protective, but a 2026 meta-analysis of 29 studies found no significant pooled link between social support and perinatal depression ([Peng et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42420901/)), so the plan should not promise that family care prevents depression. Women's reasons for choosing birth attendants also carry a design lesson: in Kano and Oyo they cited mistreatment in facilities against "compassionate, women-centered, and respectful care" at home ([Yalley and Sanusi Gumbi](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42232019/)).

Kenya and Ghana need their own entries. Among the Kalenjin, 68% avoid eggs during pregnancy to keep the baby small ([Riang'a et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5445501/)), and 59.6% of Maasai and Samburu birth attendants surveyed would feed blood to a woman haemorrhaging after birth ([Pan African Medical Journal](https://www.panafrican-med-journal.com/content/series/25/2/13/full/)). Ghanaian women often use herbs alongside prescribed drugs without telling health workers ([Peprah et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6419816)), and a national report in December 2025 named herbal medicine in pregnancy and reliance on birth attendants as risk factors for maternal death ([Graphic](https://graphiconline.com/news/general-news/upper-east-volta-record-highest-maternal-mortality-as-regional-gaps-widen.html)). The library therefore needs tags by country, region, and ethnic group. Policy supports this stance. WHO's Traditional Medicine Strategy 2025–2034 prioritises evidence, safety, and integration ([WHO](https://who.int/publications/i/item/9789240113176)), and Nigeria's Traditional Medicine Policy commits government to integration rather than prohibition ([Policy Vault Africa](https://policyvault.africa/?p=71299)). Because language models are biased against traditional remedies and easily misled about them, this content should be written and signed off by clinicians and released under a Creative Commons licence, with the model retrieving it and not generating it.

The messages are cheap. On the researchers' arithmetic, 80 SMS across a 40-week pregnancy cost ₦160–480 (US$0.12–0.36), the same number of WhatsApp utility templates US$0.54–0.81, and 80 one-minute voice calls about ₦1,200 (US$0.90). Extending the arithmetic, 200 bulk SMS across the full 1,000 days would cost roughly ₦390–1,300 (US$0.29–0.98). The traps are classification and the helpdesk. Meta prices marketing templates at roughly **eight times** utility templates, so care-plan messages must be registered as account-specific utility messages, and SMS reminders must be sent as consented transactional traffic to reach the many numbers registered on the "do not disturb" list ([Message Central](https://www.messagecentral.com/sms-guideline/nigeria)). The researchers disagree on whether Meta prices Nigeria as its own market or within its "Rest of Africa" band, and Kenyan sources report that replies inside the 24-hour window became chargeable on 1 October 2026 while Meta's Nigerian documentation, as fetched, did not say so. The cost model should be checked against Meta's downloadable rate card before any budget is submitted.

Most of the cost lies in people and connectivity. About **38% of Nigerian mobile subscriptions were still on 2G** in October 2025 ([MEA TechWatch citing NCC](https://meatechwatch.com/2025/12/08/nigerias-teledensity-reaches-81-as-data-usage-soars-despite-rising-tariffs/)), so the health-worker app must work offline and fall back to SMS for danger-sign alerts. A 1.8GB monthly data bundle costs ₦1,500 ([TechEconomy](https://techeconomy.ng/mtn-nigeria-hikes-data-prices-by-up-to-200)), which is **30% of a CHIPS agent's monthly stipend**, so someone other than the agent must pay for data. A nurse helpdesk is the costliest component of PROMPTS, and South Africa's MomConnect spent about US$60,000 a month on SMS and USSD, three-quarters of its running costs, at South African prices ([BMJ Global Health](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5922496)). Fixed USSD costs, including a ₦500,000 MTN connection fee for a dedicated code, only pay off at scale, so a pilot should use a shared code with the platform paying for sessions. With rural female literacy at 48%, voice in Hausa and other languages, or delivery through the health worker, is required for about half the target group.

## Data, device, and health-records law all reach the same care plan

Four Nigerian regimes apply. The first is the **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023** and its General Application and Implementation Directive, in force since September 2025. Health status is sensitive data requiring express consent, a data-protection impact assessment is mandatory before large-scale processing of sensitive data, breaches must be reported to the regulator within 72 hours, and AI deployers must protect the right not to be subject to purely automated decisions and test in controlled, low-risk settings ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/privacy-protection/1700306/gaid-2025-unpacked-series-ii-data-protection-officers-mandatory-consents-and-emerging-technologies); [LawPavilion](https://blog.lawpavilion.com/ndp-act-2023-gaid-2025-a-comprehensive-guide-to-nigerias-new-data-protection-landscape)). Anyone processing the sensitive data of more than 200 people commercially must register as a data controller of major importance, at the lowest tier for ₦10,000, appoint a data protection officer, and file annual audit returns costing ₦100,000–1m ([NDPC guidance notice](https://ndpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/NDP-ACT-GAID-2025-MARCH-20-1.pdf); [DLA Piper](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2025/06/nigeria-ndpc-issues-gaid-key-compliance-insights/)). A federal court upheld the regulator's power to designate such controllers in July 2026 ([Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2026/07/28/pos-agents-must-register-with-ndpc-court-rules/)). No adequacy list exists, and contractual clauses for transfers abroad need the regulator's approval, so sending identifiable health data to an American or European model API is risky. Penalty levels under the Act were not verified in the notes.

The second is the **National Health Act 2014**, which makes all health information confidential and permits disclosure on the user's consent "in writing", by court order, or for public health ([NHA text](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig162642.pdf)). Failure to prevent unauthorised access is an offence carrying up to two years in prison or a ₦250,000 fine. Sharing a record among a CHEW, a midwife, and a referral hospital probably fits the Act's allowance for disclosure in the user's interest, but whether consent captured by SMS or recorded voice counts as "in writing" is unresolved. The third is **NAFDAC's software-as-a-medical-device guideline**, effective July 2024, which covers any app with a medical purpose, classifies risk on the IMDRF I–IV matrix, has **no carve-out for clinician-reviewed decision support and no AI-specific rules**, and issues five-year certificates after clinical evaluation ([NAFDAC](https://nafdac.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/Files/Resources/Guidelines/DR_And_R_Guidelines/Guidelines-for-Registration-of-Software-as-a-Medical-Device-SaMD-in-Nigeria.pdf)). Reminders and education fall near the bottom of that matrix, while danger-sign triage that drives referral of a pregnant woman or newborn would probably fall in Category II or III. Fees were not found. Academic critics note that NAFDAC has no rules for model drift or bias ([UNIZIK journal](https://journals.unizik.edu.ng/naujppl/article/view/8056)). The fourth is the emerging AI regime. The National AI Strategy is policy rather than law, and a pending bill would name healthcare as high-risk and require registration and impact assessments ([TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2025/11/06/nigeria-ai-bill/)); the status of that bill and of the Digital Economy and E-Governance Bill as of November 2026 is unconfirmed ([Techhive](https://www.techhiveadvisory.africa/insights/nigeria-a-2025-retrospective-and-2026-outlook)). No telemedicine statute exists, and the advertising code is read as barring promises of diagnosis by digital means ([Mondaq legal guide](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/healthcare/1623234/a-legal-guide-for-the-provision-of-health-services-in-nigeria-through-digital-channels)), so the service should be marketed as care planning and information.

Three conclusions follow. Keeping a licensed human between the AI and the clinical decision avoids the automated-decision consent trigger in Nigeria, matches Ghana's draft policy that "AI is a decision-support tool and not a replacement for clinicians", and is the main defence against negligence claims, since no Nigerian law allocates liability for clinical software. Splitting the product into an informational mother-facing module and a separately classified health-worker module limits the device footprint, and an early pre-submission meeting with NAFDAC is advisable. And all three countries push toward in-country or safeguarded hosting, which favours open-weight models that can run locally over a closed API.

## Governments and donors are the customers, and the handover plan starts on day one

Mothers cannot carry the cost. Out-of-pocket spending is **71–77% of Nigeria's health expenditure**, insurance covers 10–12% of the population ([allAfrica 2025 review](https://allafrica.com/stories/202512310097.html); [CHAI](https://www.clintonhealthaccess.org/blog/nigeria-tripled-health-insurance-coverage/)), and the poorest fifth of women have a facility delivery rate of 17.9% ([KIR](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/PR157/PR157.pdf)). The history of Nigerian maternal technology confirms the pattern. Hello Mama improved process indicators and died when Cross River declined to fund it, with an official calling it "cost-intensive" ([Nigeria Health Watch](https://sjai.nigeriahealthwatch.com/?p=4290)). Direct-to-consumer apps such as Babymigo reached many parents but published no revenue, the ventures that persist stack grants (HelpMum) or attach to payers and facilities (MomCare with Lagos's insurance agency, Helium with clinic credit), and no Nigerian maternal-health startup has verified revenue figures in the public record.

The public money is real and getting more domestic. The Basic Health Care Provision Fund pays NPHCDA-accredited facilities **₦600,000–800,000 a quarter** under its second phase, rising to about 13,000 facilities ([ICIR](https://www.icirnigeria.org/bhcpf-fg-disbursed-n32-9bn-in-first-six-months-of-2025-as-1164-facilities-remain-partly-functional/)). The World Bank's US$570m HOPE-PHC programme funds maternal and newborn care and the community financing shortfall ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2024/09/26/world-bank-approves-new-financing-for-nigeria-to-improve-health-outcomes-safety-of-dams-and-irrigation-services)). MAMII funds community and digital pillars in 172 areas. The MTN–Gates Multiplier's US$25m for 500,000 women implies about US$50 per woman across phones, data, devices, and software, on simple division. Lagos's Ilera Eko scheme had 1.3 million beneficiaries in June 2025 ([NewsExpress](https://newsexpressngr.com/news/296141/lagos-to-enforce-health-insurance-for-civil-servants-expands-maternal-care-under-ilera-eko-lashma)), and in Kaduna the Gates Foundation is paying a year's insurance for 32,000 vulnerable people, 60% of them pregnant women ([Gazette NGR](https://gazettengr.com/kaduna-gates-foundation-to-enrol-32000-vulnerable-residents-into-health-scheme/)). American aid cuts in 2025 closed 331 primary health centres in the North-East ([Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/?p=2610190)), which makes state budgets and multilaterals more important as buyers. Kenya's equivalent is the US$80m Beginnings Fund for 21 high-burden counties, which includes digital tools ([Beginnings Fund](https://beginningsfund.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/PRESS-RELEASE_The-Beginnings-Fund-Kenya-Coorperative-Agreement.pdf)), and Ghana's is the Free Primary Healthcare programme, whose first phase covers 150 underserved districts through community health compounds ([NHIS](https://nhis.gov.gh/News/prez-mahama-launches-free-primary-healthcare-policy-6237)).

The business model that fits is open source with paid implementation. The code and content would be free, and revenue would come from state contracts for deployment, hosting, training, support, and integration with DHIS2 and insurance claims, priced per facility or per local government area. As an illustration, a fee equal to 5% of a facility's quarterly BHCPF grant would be ₦30,000–40,000 (about US$23–30) a quarter. Donor programmes such as the Multiplier would buy implementation, and urban HMOs and Ilera Eko could pay per enrolled member for continuity and remote follow-up, with an optional premium tier for mothers who want a clinician on call. UNICEF itself is a channel: 18 portfolio companies hold long-term arrangements that let UNICEF country offices buy their products ([UNICEF Venture Fund](https://www.unicefventurefund.org/funding-support)). Charging northern mothers directly would fail. DawaMom's practice of paying community agents a share of subscriptions is a template for incentives, though untested at scale. Whatever the mix, every pilot contract should name the state budget line that will take over, because Hello Mama and MomConnect show that a programme survives only once government pays for it.

## Implications for the product, the UNICEF bid, and the launch order

### Build a guideline engine with an AI layer on top

The plan's clinical backbone should be deterministic. Schedules, supplement rules, and danger signs should come from the WHO antenatal and postnatal kits and Nigeria's immunisation schedule, keyed to the kits' identifiers so that the data flows into DHIS2 and FHIR-based national systems. The AI should do four bounded jobs: personalise the content and order of messages, speak to mothers in Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo, and Pidgin, triage incoming messages, and rank which mothers each health worker should call or visit next, the job with the best trial evidence behind it. Triage should run in three tiers (emergency now, same-day review, next contact), with rule-based danger-sign detection first, fixed and clinically approved templates for emergencies, a human queue with a response-time target, and validation sets that over-sample the same-day cases where models miss most. Generated answers should draw only on retrieved, approved content, with no dosing advice.

Each mother-child pair should carry one plan, shared between her phone and her health worker's register and anchored to an identifier other than her phone number, because SIM changes and phone sharing break number-keyed systems. The health-worker app should be offline-first, should encode a permission matrix by cadre that defaults to "refer" whenever the Standing Orders are unclear, and should replace the CHIPS paper checklist rather than add to it; Ghana's e-Tracker lost most of its users to double entry ([mHealth journal](https://mhealth.amegroups.org/article/view/133615/html)). Postnatal care deserves the most design effort: it has the steepest drop in Nigeria, the largest effect in the PROMPTS trial, and the lowest message exposure in Mobile Midwife. That means birth notification by the health worker, prompts for visits in the first 48 hours, Edinburgh screening at booking, in the third trimester, and at six weeks with same-day escalation for any self-harm response, and messages addressed to the grandmother who runs omugwo. The "learning from expert pushback" feature should log every override with its reason and feed it to a clinical governance board that updates the content library on a release schedule. Live model retraining would conflict with NAFDAC's expectation of change control and with the medical-software rules in Kenya. An open-weight model that can be hosted in Nigeria would satisfy the data law, the Digital Public Good test of platform independence, and Kenya's localisation pressure at once; HelpMum, HLlama, and Jacaranda's UlizaLlama show it can be done.

### Apply on 11 November with a working demo and a Nigerian story

The official deadline is **11:59 pm CET on 11 November 2026**; an aggregator's 4 November date is wrong ([UNICEF call](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/FemTechCall); [application form](https://form.jotform.com/262722015144346)). The form screens out anything other than a registered private for-profit company in a programme country with an open-source or to-be-opened solution, so the applicant should be a CAC-registered Nigerian limited company. It requires a repository link, quantitative and qualitative results from prototyping, and a pitch video of up to two minutes showing "the people building the solution, speaking for themselves, and the solution in action". Videos made with AI avatars or synthetic voices "will not be assessed". A slide-only concept will fail. Text fields allow 150 or 250 words with a minimum of about 100, and partners must be real, with "NA" if none exist.

The pitch should tick Pillar 1, whose fourth item asks for "frontier health platforms supporting continuity of care across the maternal health continuum, from conception through the first 1,000 days of a child's life, including remote care in low-bandwidth environments", almost word for word the concept. It can add Pillar 3 for maternal depression screening that feeds DHIS2 and decision support for community workers, and Pillar 2 for interoperable case management and referral. The only published scoring rubric, from a 2023 guideline the call still links, weights relevance 20, novelty 30, team 30, and budget 20; it is indicative, since the call has not confirmed it applies ([REOI guidelines](https://www.unicefventurefund.org/sites/default/files/2023-08/Request%20for%20Expression%20of%20Interest%202022%20-%20External.pdf)). Novelty should rest on what cohort 1 lacks: a shared plan across the whole 1,000 days, a CHW workflow, a Creative Commons library of graded traditional practices, and a route into MAMII and DHIS2. The call says "safety-by-design is a condition of UNICEF's investment", so the application should describe the impact assessment, consent in local languages, human review, and the plan for **publicly exposed real-time data**, which should be aggregated, de-identified counts such as women enrolled, visits completed, and referrals closed ([Venture Fund rules](https://www.unicefventurefund.org/apply-funding)). The form asks whether the company is founded by women, defined as ownership plus an active governance role, and cohort 1's shortlist was 89% woman-led; a team without such a co-founder is eligible but probably disadvantaged, an inference rather than a stated rule. No Nigerian company was in cohort 1 ([BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/technology/article/unicef-femtech-ventures-backs-11-startups/)), which may help, though no quota is stated.

The award is up to US$100,000, equity-free, paid in two or three tranches under a one-year vendor contract, possibly in cryptocurrency, with the funded code under GPL, MIT, or BSD and content under CC-BY by month six ([Venture Fund](https://www.unicefventurefund.org/funding-support)). The company keeps its other IP. The call promises 12 months of mentorship, while older Venture Fund pages say four to five months, and the call-specific figure should govern. Under the standing timeline, shortlisting and a full proposal follow in months two and three and a decision comes around month six, roughly mid-2027. If no working product can be filmed by 11 November, the honest course is to build one and apply to the next annual round, since Femtech Ventures runs to 2030.

### Launch in Kaduna, add northern Ghana, and enter Kenya through eCHIS

| Phase | Site | Timing | Entry point | Likely payer | Channel and language | Regulatory steps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nigeria: Kaduna MAMII areas | Months 0–12 | State PHC agency; CHEWs, JCHEWs, CHIPS | UNICEF grant, MTN–Gates Multiplier, MAMII and BHCPF | SMS and voice in Hausa; WhatsApp for health workers | NDPC registration, impact assessment, NAFDAC pre-submission |
| 1b | Nigeria: Lagos | Months 6–18 | Lagos insurance agency, HMOs | Insurer per member, premium tier | WhatsApp in English, Pidgin, Yoruba | As above |
| 2 | Ghana: Upper East, North East, Northern | Months 9–18 | GHS steering committee; a district Network of Practice | Free Primary Healthcare districts, donors | SMS and voice in Dagbani, Gurene, Hausa; Twi later | DPC registration; FDA classification |
| 3 | Kenya: one or two Beginnings Fund counties | From month 18 | MoH Division of Community Health and Medic | Beginnings Fund, county funds | Module inside eCHIS; SMS in Swahili | ODPC, Digital Health Agency, PPB, local hosting |

Kaduna is the best first site. It has Nigeria's largest pool of community health practitioners, one of the widest drops between antenatal contact (70.0%) and facility birth (25.9%), a fully domesticated task-shifting policy with standard operating procedures ([Pathfinder, 2025](https://www.pathfinder.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025_TSTS-Progress-Brief.pdf)), a state that has given 15% of its budget to health since 2023 ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202512040063.html)), active Gates funding, and MAMII facilities. It also avoids a head-on clash with PROMPTS in neighbouring Kano, and the two could later complement each other, with PROMPTS handling the mother's helpdesk and the planner the health worker's plan. Lagos follows as the urban, insured, WhatsApp-heavy cohort that can test paying demand.

Ghana should come second. Its northern regions combine the country's highest maternal mortality and stunting with low literacy and no Jacaranda presence, Hausa is spoken there, and the Ghana Health Service is only exploring a digital version of the paper Mother and Child Health Record Book, which already covers pregnancy to early childhood ([JICA](https://www.jica.go.jp/english/jica_ri/news/interview/2026/1581366_70693.html)). The FDA's exclusion of scheduling software also lightens the regulatory load for the plan itself. Kenya should come third and only as an integration. Its community workers already carry an open-source app on government phones, PROMPTS has more than three million users, and its rules on data localisation and medical software are the strictest of the three. A planner built on or interoperable with the Community Health Toolkit could arrive as a module through Medic and the ministry, funded by the Beginnings Fund. A standalone Kenyan launch would compete with the state.

## Conclusion

In Kaduna the product will compete above all with the CHIPS agent's paper checklist, and the absence of anyone who knows that a woman seen at 30 weeks has not been seen since. The evidence gives the AI a modest and defensible job: choosing which mother a health worker should contact next and speaking to her in her own language, while WHO's kits supply the clinical logic and clinicians write the traditional-practice library. Those two assets, a shared plan object and an openly licensed, graded library of practices, are what a funder cannot already buy from Jacaranda, DawaMom, or HLlama, and they qualify naturally as digital public goods.

Several pressures that look separate point to one architecture. The Digital Public Good test of platform independence, Nigeria's limits on transfers abroad, Kenya's localisation rules, and NAFDAC's need for change control all favour an open-weight model hosted in-country, with expert corrections fed into scheduled content releases rather than live retraining. The money points the same way. UNICEF's US$100,000 buys a credible pilot and a seat beside governments, and the measure of success at month 12 should be a Kaduna budget line or a Multiplier contract, because Nigeria has already shown, with Hello Mama, what happens to a maternal messaging service that improves care and has no one to pay for it.
